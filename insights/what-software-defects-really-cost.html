What software defects really cost.

"We do not have budget for testing" is one of the most expensive sentences in business software. Here is the arithmetic that makes it so.

9 min readSoftware testingSeptember 2026

Every organisation that has ever cut a testing budget did so for a defensible reason. The deadline was immovable. The build had overrun. Testing was the only line item that looked optional, because it is the only one that produces no visible feature.

The decision looks rational right up until the arithmetic is done properly. It almost never is, because the costs of a defect do not appear on the project's budget line. They appear in support, in operations, in the finance team's manual corrections, and occasionally in a regulator's inbox.

The escalation curve

The most durable finding in software engineering research is that the cost of fixing a defect rises sharply with how late it is found. The exact multipliers vary with the study, the methodology and the era — anyone quoting a precise universal figure is overselling it — but the direction and rough magnitude are consistent across four decades and across wildly different kinds of organisation.

The shape looks roughly like this.

Found duringRelative costWhat fixing it actually involves
Requirements & design1×An afternoon and a conversation. Change a diagram.
Development~5×Rework of code, re-review, re-merge.
Independent testing~10×Code change, retest, regression cycle, release delay.
User acceptance~15×All of the above plus stakeholder time and reputational cost internally.
Production~30×+Hotfix, emergency release, support load, data correction, customer communication, possible reporting obligation.

Treat those multipliers as an order of magnitude rather than a precise measurement. The commercially important point is that the curve is steep and it never bends the other way.

Working through a real-shaped example

Consider a mid-sized South African business building a customer portal. Budget R850 000, six months, an internal team plus a contractor. Testing was scoped at R120 000 and was cut to R30 000 in month four to recover time lost to an integration that took longer than expected.

That saved R90 000. Here is what the following ninety days cost.

ConsequenceWorkingCost
Emergency hotfix releases4 releases × ~R18 000 in developer, review and deployment timeR72 000
Support load~340 extra tickets × 22 minutes × loaded hourly costR41 000
Manual data correctionFinance staff correcting mis-posted transactions, 3 weeks part-timeR38 000
Delayed second phaseTeam held on stabilisation for 5 weeks instead of buildingR190 000
Customer churnConservative: 1.5% of portal users abandoned during the unstable periodNot modelled
Total, excluding churnR341 000

A R90 000 saving produced R341 000 of cost — a net loss of R251 000, before anything is assumed about lost customers. And the delayed phase two, the largest single line, is the one nobody attributes to the testing decision, because by then it reads as a resourcing problem.

The attribution problem

This is why the mistake repeats. The saving is visible, immediate, and credited to the person who made it. The cost is diffuse, delayed, and lands in someone else's budget. Nothing in a typical reporting structure connects the two.

The costs that do not appear in any budget

The table above only counts what is measurable. Three larger costs usually go unrecorded.

Fear. In a codebase without a trustworthy test suite, every change is risky, so every change gets slower. Teams stop refactoring. Small improvements stop happening because nobody wants to be the person who broke checkout. The velocity loss is enormous and completely invisible on any report.

Attrition. Good engineers leave systems that are frightening to work on. Replacing a senior developer in South Africa costs somewhere between six and twelve months of salary once recruitment, notice periods and the ramp-up of their replacement are counted.

Regulatory exposure. Since POPIA became enforceable, a defect that exposes personal information is no longer just a bug. It is a potentially notifiable incident with statutory deadlines, board visibility and a regulator who can issue an enforcement notice. A broken access-control check is now a compliance event.

What a defensible testing budget looks like

There is no universal percentage, and firms that quote one are guessing. What is defensible is sizing the investment against what failure would actually cost.

  • Low consequence of failure — an internal tool, a marketing site, a system with a manual fallback. Automated unit tests, a single exploratory cycle before release, and accept the residual risk consciously.
  • Moderate consequence — a customer-facing system where downtime is embarrassing but survivable. Test strategy, an automated regression suite on the critical journeys, a performance baseline, and independent testing before major releases.
  • High consequence — money moves, personal information is processed, or a regulator is interested. Embedded quality engineering from the first sprint, continuous automated regression, performance and security testing, and formal evidence retained for audit.

Notice that the variable is consequence, not project size. A small system that moves money deserves more testing than a large internal reporting tool.

Where the money is best spent

If your budget is genuinely constrained, this is the order that buys the most certainty per rand.

  1. A risk workshop, before anything is built. Two hours identifying what would actually hurt. Cheapest quality intervention that exists, and it redirects every subsequent decision.
  2. Automated tests on the critical path only. Not 90% coverage. The eight journeys that must never break, tested on every commit.
  3. One independent exploratory cycle before release. A person who did not write it, actively trying to break it, for a week. Consistently finds the defects automation never will.
  4. A performance baseline. Cheap to establish, and it turns "the system feels slow" into a number you can act on.
  5. Everything else. Broader automation, accessibility, load testing at scale — valuable, but after the four above.

The counter-argument, taken seriously

There is a real case against heavy testing, and it deserves a straight answer rather than dismissal.

For an early-stage product still searching for a market, the dominant risk is building something nobody wants. Extensive testing of a feature that will be deleted in six weeks is waste. Move fast, keep the blast radius small, accept that things will break, and make sure you can roll back quickly.

That argument holds precisely until one of three things becomes true: real customers depend on it, real money flows through it, or real personal information is stored in it. Most organisations pass all three thresholds without ever revisiting the decision they made when none of them applied. That is the failure — not the original choice, but never reconsidering it.

Compliance

POPIA is not a privacy policy.

Most South African businesses believe they are compliant because someone pasted a privacy policy onto the website in 2021. Here is the rest of the list.

11 min readCyber securityUpdated September 2026

There is a specific conversation we have had more times than any other. It goes like this. We ask a company whether they are POPIA compliant. They say yes. We ask what that consists of. They say they have a privacy policy on the website.

A privacy policy is one obligation out of roughly a dozen, and on its own it is closer to evidence of a problem than evidence of compliance — because it demonstrates that somebody thought about POPIA once, in 2021, and then stopped.

This is not a legal opinion and it does not replace one. It is a practical map of what the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 actually requires of an ordinary South African business, written by engineers who spend their time implementing the technical half of it.

Start here: does POPIA apply to you?

Almost certainly, yes. POPIA applies to any responsible party — a person or organisation that determines why and how personal information is processed — that is domiciled in South Africa, or that processes personal information here.

There is no small-business exemption. If you have a contact form, a newsletter, a CRM, employee records, a CCTV camera at reception, or a spreadsheet of customer phone numbers, you are processing personal information and the Act applies. The threshold for "processing" is deliberately wide: collecting, storing, using, sharing, and even deleting all count.

Personal information also covers more than most people assume. It includes information about a juristic person — a company — which is unusual internationally and routinely missed. Your B2B client database is personal information under South African law even where it holds no individual's details at all.

The eight conditions for lawful processing

POPIA is structured around eight conditions. Every other obligation flows from these, so it is worth understanding what they actually demand rather than treating them as legal furniture.

  1. Accountability. You must be able to demonstrate compliance, not merely assert it. In practice this means documentation.
  2. Processing limitation. Collect the minimum necessary, lawfully, and with a valid justification — consent is only one of several, and often the weakest.
  3. Purpose specification. Collect for a specific, defined purpose, and say what it is at the time of collection.
  4. Further processing limitation. Do not later use the information for something incompatible with that original purpose.
  5. Information quality. Keep it accurate, complete and current.
  6. Openness. Maintain the documentation, tell people you are processing their information, and have a PAIA manual.
  7. Security safeguards. Appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures — and a duty to notify when they fail.
  8. Data subject participation. People may ask what you hold about them, and may require correction or deletion.

The one most businesses fail

Condition 1, accountability, is the quiet killer. Many organisations are substantively fine — they do not over-collect, they secure things reasonably, they would honour a deletion request. But asked to demonstrate it to a regulator, they have nothing written down. In an enforcement context, undocumented compliance is very close to non-compliance.

The obligations checklist

Work through these in order. Items one to four are foundational and most organisations are missing at least two.

1. Appoint and register an Information Officer

Every private body has an Information Officer. By default it is the head of the organisation — the CEO, the managing director, or in a small company the owner. You may delegate the work to a Deputy Information Officer, but accountability stays at the top.

Registration with the Information Regulator is free, done online, and takes about twenty minutes. Failing to register is one of the most common and most easily fixed gaps, and it is the first thing a regulator will check, because verifying it costs them nothing.

2. Compile a PAIA manual

The Promotion of Access to Information Act requires every private body to have a manual describing what records it holds and how a person may request access to them. POPIA amended PAIA so that the manual now carries data-protection content too.

The manual must be available at your principal place of business and, in practice, on your website. It is not long, it is not expensive, and its absence is trivially easy for anyone to spot — including a competitor, a disgruntled ex-employee or a regulator doing a sweep.

3. Establish a lawful basis for each processing activity

Consent is one of six justifications, and it is the most fragile one because it can be withdrawn at any time. The others are frequently stronger and less administratively painful:

  • Performance of a contract with the data subject
  • Compliance with a legal obligation
  • Protection of a legitimate interest of the data subject
  • Performance of a public law duty by a public body
  • Pursuit of the legitimate interests of the responsible party or a third party

You do not need consent to send a client an invoice; that is contractual. You do generally need it to add them to a marketing list — section 69 restricts direct electronic marketing to existing customers about similar products, or to people who have opted in, and it allows one approach to request that consent. One.

4. Maintain a record of processing activities

What you collect, why, where it lives, who can see it, who you share it with, how long you keep it, and how it is destroyed. A spreadsheet is a perfectly acceptable format. The point is that it exists and is current.

This is the document that makes every other obligation tractable. Without it you cannot answer a data subject access request, you cannot assess a breach, and you cannot demonstrate accountability.

5. Put operator agreements in place

An operator is anyone who processes personal information on your behalf: your hosting provider, your payroll bureau, your CRM vendor, your accountant, your outsourced development studio. Section 21 requires a written contract obliging them to maintain the same security safeguards you are required to maintain, and to notify you immediately of any compromise.

Most organisations have dozens of operators and written agreements with almost none of them. Start with the ones holding the most sensitive information.

6. Define retention periods and actually delete things

Personal information may not be kept longer than necessary for the purpose it was collected for, unless another law requires retention. Tax records have statutory retention periods. The CVs from a hiring round in 2022 do not.

"We keep everything forever because storage is cheap" is a POPIA problem and also a security problem: data you have deleted cannot be stolen.

7. Have a breach response procedure before you need one

Section 22 requires notification to the Information Regulator and to affected data subjects as soon as reasonably possible after you have reasonable grounds to believe personal information has been accessed or acquired by an unauthorised person. There is limited room to delay while law enforcement or the Regulator determines whether notification would impede an investigation.

You cannot write that procedure during the incident. You need, in advance: who decides that a breach has occurred, who contacts the Regulator, what the notification says, who tells affected people, and who talks to the press. Add a tabletop exercise and you will discover the gaps cheaply.

8. Implement appropriate security safeguards

Section 19 requires appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures, having regard to generally accepted information security practice. It is deliberately not prescriptive, which people sometimes read as permissive. It is not — it means the standard rises as practice improves.

In practice, for a mid-sized South African business, "appropriate" in 2026 looks something like: multi-factor authentication everywhere, encryption in transit and at rest, least-privilege access with periodic review, patching within a defined window, tested backups, logging and alerting, security awareness training, and independent testing of anything internet-facing.

The website-specific obligations

Since this is the part people think about first, here is what actually applies to a website.

ElementWhat POPIA requires
Privacy noticeIdentify the responsible party and Information Officer, state what you collect and why, name third parties you share with, state retention periods, explain data subject rights and how to exercise them.
Contact and enquiry formsCollect only what you need, state the purpose at the point of collection, and obtain consent where consent is your justification. Do not pre-tick the box.
Marketing opt-inA separate, unticked, clearly worded opt-in. Bundling marketing consent into a contact form is not valid consent.
Cookies and analyticsNon-essential cookies need a lawful basis. A cookieless, privacy-friendly analytics tool removes the problem entirely and is usually more accurate anyway.
PAIA manualPublished and reachable — conventionally in the footer.
Third-party embedsFonts, maps, chat widgets and video players loaded from foreign servers transmit visitor IP addresses abroad. Self-host where you reasonably can.
Transfers outside South AfricaSection 72 permits cross-border transfer only under specific conditions. Most cloud services are outside the country — know which, and on what basis.

A note on this website

We hold ourselves to the same list. This site loads no third-party fonts, no external tracking scripts, no chat widget and no advertising pixels. Our contact form collects only what we need to reply to you, and says so. It is not a moral position — it is simply cheaper to be compliant than to explain why you are not.

What can the Regulator actually do?

Enforcement has moved well past the guidance phase. The Information Regulator can investigate on complaint or on its own initiative, issue an enforcement notice requiring specific action, and refer matters for prosecution. Failure to comply with an enforcement notice is an offence, and the Act provides for administrative fines and, for certain offences, imprisonment.

In practice the more common damage is quieter and arrives sooner: a large client's vendor assessment asks for your Information Officer registration and your operator agreements, you cannot produce them, and you lose the contract. That happens weekly in South African procurement and it never makes the news.

A realistic order of work

If you are starting from close to nothing, this sequence gets you the most protection for the least effort.

  1. This week. Register your Information Officer. Publish a PAIA manual and a privacy notice that reflects reality rather than a template. Remove any pre-ticked consent boxes.
  2. This month. Build the record of processing activities. Work out where personal information actually lives, including the shadow copies in inboxes and spreadsheets. Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere.
  3. This quarter. Operator agreements with your top ten suppliers. A retention schedule, and one actual deletion run against it. A written breach procedure, tested once around a table.
  4. This year. Independent security testing of anything internet-facing. Staff awareness training. A review cycle, because compliance is a state you maintain rather than a project you finish.

This article is general information, not legal advice. POPIA obligations vary with what you process and why, and a qualified attorney should review anything consequential.

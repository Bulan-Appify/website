AI automation without the hype.

Most AI pilots die for an unglamorous reason: nobody checked whether the process was worth automating. Here is how to check.

10 min readAI & automationSeptember 2026

There is enormous pressure on executives right now to have an AI story. Boards ask about it. Competitors announce things. Vendors arrive with demonstrations that are genuinely impressive for eight minutes.

That pressure produces a recognisable artefact: the pilot that demos beautifully, never reaches production, and quietly vanishes from the roadmap about eighteen months later. Nobody writes a post-mortem, because nobody wants to be the person who says the emperor's assistant had no clothes.

The projects that do work share a profile so boring that it rarely gets presented at conferences. This article is about recognising that profile.

Four questions that kill most pilots

Ask these before anyone writes a line of code. A candidate that cannot answer at least three convincingly should not be funded.

1. Is the volume actually there?

Forty instances a month is a spreadsheet problem, not an AI problem. Automation earns back its build and running cost through repetition, and the maths is unforgiving: a process running twice a week will not repay a R125 000 build inside any horizon a CFO cares about.

Count the instances before you do anything else. People systematically overestimate the frequency of processes that are annoying and underestimate the frequency of processes that are routine.

2. Is a good-enough answer genuinely useful?

This is the question that kills the most projects, usually late and expensively. Language models are probabilistic. They will be wrong some percentage of the time, and that percentage will never be zero.

If a process demands perfect accuracy with no human review and no tolerance for error, deterministic rules and validation beat models — every time, at lower cost, with an audit trail. Ask, honestly: is 95% accuracy with a human checking the flagged 5% genuinely better than what happens today? Frequently the answer is a resounding yes, because what happens today is a tired person doing it at 16:40 on a Friday. But sometimes the answer is no, and it is far cheaper to discover that in a meeting.

3. Can you measure whether it worked?

Handling time. Error rate. Backlog age. Cost per transaction. Pick one, measure it before you build, and agree the target in writing.

Without a baseline you cannot prove value, which means the project has no defence when budgets tighten. A startling proportion of AI initiatives are cancelled not because they failed but because nobody could demonstrate that they had succeeded.

4. What happens when it is wrong?

Every automation needs a defined failure mode before it goes live: a confidence threshold, a human review queue, an audit trail, and a named person accountable for the outcome.

Where the decision materially affects a person, this is not just good practice. Section 71 of POPIA restricts decisions taken solely by automated means that have legal consequences for someone or affect them substantially. Credit decisions, employment screening and claims adjudication all live squarely in that territory.

The fifth question, asked too late

Will anyone actually use it? The most common cause of death for internal AI tools is not accuracy — it is adoption. If the people who do the work were not consulted during design, the tool loses to their existing spreadsheet, and it loses quietly, so you find out six months later from a usage report.

The five shapes worth doing first

These recur across sectors because they share the right profile: high volume, tolerant of a good-enough answer with human review, easy to measure, and with an obvious failure mode.

1. Document extraction and validation

Invoices, claims, applications, statements, delivery notes, identity documents. Somebody is reading these and typing the contents into a system, hundreds or thousands of times a month.

This is the highest-confidence automation available to most businesses. The output is structured and therefore checkable, confidence scores route uncertain cases to a human, and the baseline — minutes per document — is trivially measurable. Payback is frequently under nine months.

Warning sign: if the documents are wildly inconsistent and low-volume, the exception handling costs more than the automation saves.

2. Internal knowledge assistants

A grounded assistant over your own policies, procedures, contracts and precedent, which cites the source paragraph and respects existing access permissions.

The value is highest where institutional knowledge is concentrated in a few overloaded people. New staff get answers without interrupting a senior colleague, and the senior colleague gets their afternoons back.

Warning sign: if your documentation is out of date, contradictory or scattered across four systems, the assistant will confidently surface the wrong version. Fix the documents first — and that clean-up has value regardless of whether the assistant ever ships.

3. Customer support triage

Not replacing your support team. Classifying incoming queries, routing them correctly, drafting responses for genuinely repetitive questions, and handing anything complicated to a human with the full context already assembled.

The measurable win is first-response time and the proportion of tickets resolved without escalation.

Warning sign: automation that makes it harder to reach a person destroys more value than it creates. The hand-off must be one obvious click, always available.

4. Reconciliation and exception handling

Matching records across systems that were never designed to agree — payments to invoices, deliveries to orders, stock counts to ledgers. The rules handle the clean 85%; the interesting work is the rest.

This is often better served by good old-fashioned rules with a model only for the genuinely ambiguous cases. Being honest about that split is the difference between a system that works and an expensive demonstration.

5. Structured report generation

Recurring reports that a person currently assembles by pulling numbers from several systems and writing the same commentary with different figures. Monthly management packs, compliance returns, client status reports.

The data comes from your systems deterministically; the model handles narrative and formatting. A human reviews and signs. Straightforward to measure, straightforward to check.

Three things to be sceptical of

"We will train our own model." Almost certainly wrong for a business problem. Training from scratch is a research activity. Even fine-tuning is usually premature — better retrieval, better prompting and better data preparation solve most quality problems at a fraction of the cost, and without freezing you to a model generation that will be superseded within a year.

"It will replace the team." In practice, well-designed automation removes the repetitive fraction of several jobs rather than the whole of any one. Plan for redeployment rather than headcount reduction, and say so early — a workforce that suspects it is automating itself out of a job will not help you build the thing, and their cooperation is what makes it accurate.

"The demo worked, so the system will work." A demo runs on chosen examples. Production runs on the document that was photographed at an angle, in poor light, with a coffee ring on it. The gap between those two worlds is where most of the engineering effort actually lives, and any estimate that ignores it is wrong by a factor of several.

What a sensible first project looks like

Pick one process. Measure the current baseline properly for two weeks. Build the narrowest useful version with a human review queue from day one. Run it in parallel with the manual process for a month and compare. Only then decide whether to widen the scope.

That sequence is slower than a vendor's roadmap and dramatically more likely to end with something still running in two years. It also gives you an honest internal number for what AI automation actually returns in your organisation — which is worth more than the first project itself, because every subsequent decision gets easier.
